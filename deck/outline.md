# Presentation outline: The Price of Patience

**For:** a 10 to 12 minute talk, 10 slides, roughly one minute per slide.
**Tone:** plain spoken. No technical vocabulary that needs defining on screen.
**Rule for whoever builds this:** everything below comes from the paper. Do not add
statistics, cases or claims that are not here.

---

## The argument in one paragraph

When a foreign state leaves software sitting inside another country's power grid or
water system, analysts read it as a warning that an attack is coming. That reading
depends on one assumption: staying hidden for years is expensive, so anyone who bothers
must be serious. This talk shows that governments do not actually behave as if they
believe this, that the activity has never once led to a successful attack, and that AI
may be making defence stronger rather than attack. It ends with two possible
explanations and leaves the question open.

---

## Slide 1. Title

- **Title:** The Price of Patience
- **Subtitle:** What foreign code inside our power grids really tells us, and what AI does to that message
- **Footer line:** A study of sixteen works on cyber conflict, 2018 to 2026

*Say:* one sentence naming the object, software left sitting inside civilian
infrastructure, and the question the talk asks.

---

## Slide 2. An intrusion that made no sense as spying

- May 2023. The United States and its Five Eyes partners named China as the source of a
  campaign called **Volt Typhoon**.
- Found inside energy, water, communications and transport networks, and in systems
  supporting the American base on Guam.
- They took almost nothing. A spy steals. These operators sat still.

**Pull quote, use it verbatim:**
> "There is absolutely no intelligence to be gathered by putting malicious code in
> critical infrastructure networks."
> — General Paul Nakasone, then head of US Cyber Command

---

## Slide 3. So analysts read it as a warning

The reasoning, in three steps:

1. **Staying hidden is hard.** Years inside a defended network takes skill and patience. It is not cheap.
2. **So the act carries meaning.** People do not pay a high price for nothing.
3. **Therefore: preparation.** It is read as a state getting ready for a fight it expects.

**Closing line on the slide:** This whole reading rests on one word: *expensive.*

*Note:* the technical name for this is costly signalling. Do not put that phrase on the
slide.

---

## Slide 4. But governments do not act as if they believe it

**This is the most important slide. Give it the most room and the most time.**

Two columns, side by side:

| | Volt Typhoon | Salt Typhoon |
|---|---|---|
| What it was | Code left inside power, water and transport systems | Spying on phone companies and call records |
| What the US did | **A technical advisory** | **Treasury sanctions** |
| | No sanctions. No allied statement. | Named entities. Real penalties. |

**Bottom line:** Same country. Same period. Same victim. The lesser act was punished;
the graver one was not.

*Small source line:* Volt Typhoon coding from Baram (2026), a study of twenty joint
attribution cases. Salt Typhoon sanctions from the US Treasury.

---

## Slide 5. Three reasons the message never lands

1. **Nobody notices.** These operations leave no wreckage and no pictures. Nothing forces a government to act.
2. **The sender denies it.** China calls the accusation disinformation. A warning nobody admits sending is hard to receive.
3. **It reaches the wrong desk.** The response is written for network engineers, not for the people who decide on war and peace.

**Closing line:** A threat only works if it is seen, believed, and read by the right
people. This one fails all three tests.

---

## Slide 6. And it has never actually led anywhere

Three cases, and that is the whole record:

| Case | When | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Russia inside US infrastructure | 2018 to today | Still sitting there. Seven years, never used. |
| Volt Typhoon | 2023 to today | Still sitting there. Never used. |
| Russia inside Ukraine | 2021 to 2022 | Used in April 2022. Ukrainian defenders stopped it. |

**Big number on the slide: 0** — cases where this led to a successful attack.

**Closing line:** Everything the field says about what this activity means rests on no
successful examples.

*Optional detail if asked:* in Ukraine the malware would have cut power to two million
people. No outages occurred.

---

## Slide 7. Does AI favour the attacker? Probably not yet

1. **Attack tools are still assisted, not automatic.** AI helps scout targets and adjust
   tools. Fully automatic attack systems remain, in the literature's own word,
   theoretical.
2. **Almost all the AI work is defensive.** The research being published is about
   spotting intruders faster, not replacing them.
3. **Defenders currently cannot see.** Intrusions go unnoticed for 197 days on average,
   and roughly three quarters are spotted by outsiders rather than the victim. Better
   detection makes hiding harder, not easier.

**Closing box:** In the one real war we can study, defence held. But that was Ukraine,
backed by Western agencies and by Microsoft, Google and Cisco. A weaker defender would
give a different answer.

---

## Slide 8. So which is it?

Two explanations, side by side:

**A. The warning is fading.** It used to mean something, because staying hidden was
expensive. AI makes it cheaper, so the meaning drains away.
*Predicts: the gap between what we say and what we do keeps widening.*

**B. There never was a warning.** It was never meant to say anything. Staying quiet and
below the line that triggers a response was always the point.
*Predicts: the gap stays the same, because it was never about cost.*

**Closing line:** The evidence so far fits B at least as well as A. That is
uncomfortable, because our doctrine assumes A.

*Be honest on this slide: the paper does not decide between them.*

---

## Slide 9. Two risks, pointing opposite ways

**If we over-read it.** We treat routine, automated activity as a warning of war, and
punish something that meant nothing. Given how tangled cyber, space and nuclear systems
are, a limited response can run far past what anyone intended.

**If we under-read it.** Real preparation for an attack gets handed to network engineers
and filed as a maintenance problem. The difference between how Volt Typhoon and Salt
Typhoon were answered suggests this may already be happening.

**Closing line:** Getting this wrong is expensive in both directions. That is why the
question deserves an answer.

---

## Slide 10. What this study adds

1. It puts two findings side by side that nobody had joined: what analysts say this
   activity means, and how governments actually respond to it.
2. It questions the assumption underneath the standard reading, that staying hidden is
   expensive, and asks what AI does to it.
3. It records a fact the field has not faced. There is no case where this led to a
   successful attack.

**Final line, deliver it slowly and stop:**
> We have been reading a message that may never have been sent.

## Design direction

- **Colours:** deep navy as the dominant colour, white background for the middle slides,
  one warm amber used only where something is a warning. Do not use a generic blue.
- **Structure:** dark title slide, light content slides, dark closing slide.
- **Motif:** small numbered circles, repeated wherever there is a list.
- **Fonts:** a serif for headings, a clean sans for body. Headings 32 to 44pt, body 14
  to 16pt.
- **No charts.** One large number on slide 6 is the only data display.
- **No accent lines under titles and no coloured stripes down the edges.**
- Leave white space. Two columns or three cards per slide is enough.

## Things to avoid

- Do not put these words on the slides: costly signalling, offence-defence balance,
  pre-positioning, agentic autonomy. Say them out loud if you like, but the audience
  should not have to read them.
- Do not add cases, numbers or quotes beyond what is above.
- Do not add a methodology slide.
