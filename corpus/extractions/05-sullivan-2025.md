# Extraction 05 — Sullivan (2025) ★ CRITICAL — complicates the thesis

## Full citation (APA 7)
Sullivan, S. (2025). Toward clarity in cyber's "fog of law". *The Cyber Defense
Review, 10*(1), 59–71. https://doi.org/10.55682/cdr/v8z4-sxne

Author affiliation: Army Cyber Institute and Department of Law, West Point.

## Research objective
To explain the persistent legal ambiguity ("fog of law") governing state cyber
operations, and to argue for recalibration of US legal positions — principally by
revising the cyber operations chapter of the DoD *Law of War Manual*.

## Theoretical framework
- **Rules vs standards** jurisprudence (Schlag; Cohen; Townley).
- **Cyber deterrence theory** (2015 DoD strategy) versus **Cyber Persistence Theory**
  (2018 DoD strategy; Fischerkeller/Goldman/Harknett lineage via Grzegorzewski 2023).
- Customary international law formation via unilateral acts (Hakimi 2014).

## Methodology
Doctrinal legal analysis of the DoD *Law of War Manual* (2015, updated 2023),
situated against US cyber strategy documents (2015, 2018).

## Cases
**Volt Typhoon** and **Salt Typhoon** (both in passing); Stuxnet (as a cost contrast).

## ★★ CORE PASSAGE — THE COST OF CYBER OPERATIONS (directly bears on the thesis)
> "cyber operations enable States to pursue this diverse portfolio of activities
> **inexpensively**. More sophisticated cyber operations, such as Stuxnet, require
> significant monetary investment, **but most do not** (Gervais 2012). Effective cyber
> operations are not limited to the world's wealthiest States. **The low cost of cyber
> operations extends far beyond the financial realm.** Effective cyber operations can
> also render murky, if not totally escape, detection."

## ★★ CORE PASSAGE — low political salience defeats response
> "cyber operations rarely possess sufficient political salience to activate political
> institutions to punish the perpetrator in any way that might provoke meaningful
> blowback (Davis 2014). Cyber operations rarely create the wrenching imagery of
> kinetic armed conflict… Instead, the most effective and worrisome cyber operations,
> **such as Volt Typhoon and Salt Typhoon, lurk in the shadows**, compromising entire
> populations while leaving little trace."

## ★★ CORE PASSAGE — operations as tacit signalling
> "Repeated cyber engagement between adversaries offers affirmative opportunities to
> **communicate competing conceptions of what is acceptable State behavior** and what
> is not. This occurs both within the offensive and the defensive dimension. For
> example, 'defend forward' operations to dismantle specific foreign threats and not
> others **signal to an adversary** a sense of the types of most threatening or
> inappropriate cyber threats. This **'tacit bargaining'** … will ultimately resonate
> along legal lines."

## ★ Attribution: technical vs political
> "even when technical attribution is possible, political attribution rarely follows.
> Political attribution, where the victimized State publicly identifies the
> perpetrating State, is subject to political considerations that may counsel against
> attribution (Eichensehr 2020)."

## ★ The deterrence → persistence shift and its signalling consequences
> "While deterrence is reactive and punitive, persistence engagement is proactively
> preventive. As a result, deterrence is episodic in nature, while persistence
> theory's defining trait is the continuous operations that it requires… **Whereas
> deterrence theory places a premium on the value of flexibility and strategic
> ambiguity, the constant contact between States in persistent engagement both
> facilitates and benefits from clarity over ambiguity.**"

Sullivan also records that ambiguity in the 2015 Manual was *deliberate* — a
deterrence-era design choice — and quotes Nakasone (2021) that adversaries are "taking
full advantage of present legal ambiguities."

## Principal findings
Legal ambiguity in cyber is a deliberate strategic choice, now misaligned with the
shift to persistent engagement. Treaty-making is "practically impossible"; customary
law formation is blocked by attribution problems and the absence of visible state
practice. Unilateral clarification via the Manual is the viable path.

## Policy recommendations
Revise the Manual's cyber operations chapter; issue unilateral and regional statements
to seed customary norms; align legal posture with persistent engagement.

## Limitations acknowledged
Standard US Army disclaimer. Notes (fn 13) that the failure of deterrence *policy*
does not itself condemn deterrence *theory* — a careful concession.

## RELEVANCE TO RQ — critical assessment

**AI engagement: none.** The word `autonomous` appears once, in a bibliography entry
(Heller 2023, on autonomous weapons). No engagement with AI, agentic systems, or
automation in the body. **Five for five: no corpus paper connects autonomy to strategy.**

**★ THIS PAPER COMPLICATES THE WORKING THESIS — productively.**

The draft argument holds that pre-positioning signals intent *because persistence is
costly* (Guttieri's "technical competence and strategic patience"), and that AI
autonomy will erode that cost and thus the signal.

**Sullivan asserts the opposite baseline: cyber operations are *already* cheap.**
"Most do not" require significant investment; low cost "extends far beyond the
financial realm."

This is a genuine intra-corpus tension between two papers **in the same issue of the
same journal**, both from the Army Cyber Institute. It must be addressed, not elided.

**Resolution (my synthesis).** The two claims are reconcilable if cost is disaggregated:
- Sullivan's claim concerns the **cost of entry** — mounting an effective operation.
- Guttieri's claim concerns the **cost of undetected multi-year persistence** in
  hardened critical infrastructure — a different and much higher bar.

The signalling inference rests on the *second*, not the first. Pre-positioning signals
intent not because intrusion is hard, but because *staying* is. This sharpens the
thesis rather than defeating it: **the variable AI autonomy threatens is the cost of
sustained, undetected presence at scale**, not the cost of initial access.

The paper should state this disaggregation explicitly. A reviewer who knows both CDR
articles will otherwise spot the tension first.

**★ Second major contribution: tacit bargaining.** Sullivan supplies a corpus-internal
theory of *operations as signals* — "defend forward operations to dismantle specific
foreign threats and not others signal to an adversary." This is the signalling
mechanism the argument needs, and it comes from within the corpus rather than from
supplementary literature. It also runs in the *defensive* direction, which extends the
RQ: if defensive operations signal too, then automated defensive response degrades
that channel as well.

**★ Third contribution: the salience problem.** Volt Typhoon-type operations "lurk in
the shadows… leaving little trace" and lack the political salience to trigger response.
This is a *reception* problem: a signal that generates no domestic political response
cannot function coercively. Combined with Singh et al.'s (2025) finding that the PRC
denies the campaign outright, the corpus now documents **two independent failure modes
for pre-positioning-as-signal: non-reception and denial.**

**Onward citations needed (not in corpus):** Eichensehr (2020) on attribution law and
politics; Gervais (2012) on cyber operation costs; Davis (2014) on deterrence and
salience; Corn (2018) on gray-zone; Fischerkeller/Goldman/Harknett, *Cyber Persistence
Theory*.
