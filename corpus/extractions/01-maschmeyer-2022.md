# Extraction 01 — Maschmeyer (2022/2023)

## Full citation (APA 7)
Maschmeyer, L. (2023). Subversion, cyber operations, and reverse structural power
in world politics. *European Journal of International Relations, 29*(1), 79–103.
https://doi.org/10.1177/13540661221117051
(Published online 2022; issue-dated 2023.)

## Research objective
To develop a theory of subversion as "reverse structural power" that explains how
states project power through ICTs more subtly than militarised conceptions of cyber
power predict.

## Research question (as stated)
Not stated as a formal question. The animating puzzle is explicit: "why are the most
capable actors with the most advanced technology also among the most vulnerable?"

## Theoretical framework
Integrates Intelligence Studies and IR structural-power theory. Subversion reverses
the benefits of structural position into harms by secretly exploiting vulnerabilities
in structures. Traditional subversion targets social structures; cyber operations
target sociotechnical structures (ICTs embedded in societies).

## Methodology
Conceptual/theory-building article, followed by a **plausibility probe** (author's own
term) — a single case vignette, not a hypothesis test.

## Case study
2016 US Presidential Election interference campaign.

## Key concepts
Subversion; structural power; reverse structural power; sociotechnical structures;
capability–vulnerability paradox.

## Major arguments
1. The 2016 campaign used no novel "weapon"; it integrated traditional and
   sociotechnical means of subversion.
2. Cyber operations are means of subversion, not of warfare.
3. **Offence–defence theory is explicitly rejected as the explanation** for the
   capability–vulnerability paradox.

## VERBATIM — the single offence–defence passage (p. ~91)
> "Conventional wisdom attempts to explain this paradox militarily through
> offense–defense theory, diagnosing a strong offensive advantage for cyber weapons
> (Betz and Stevens, 2011: 39; Lynn, 2010; Nye, 2011: 125). This theory faces
> significant theoretical and empirical challenges, however (Gartzke and Lindsay,
> 2015; Libicki, 2009; Slayton, 2017). More recent work aims to explain this
> 'capability vulnerability paradox' through resource dependence, drawing parallels
> to previous revolutions in military affairs (Schneider, 2019). Yet, as discussed,
> military affairs are precisely not where cyber operations have been most relevant."

## Principal findings
The capability–vulnerability paradox is resolved by reverse structural power: the
greater an actor's structural power, the greater the harm subversion can inflict.
The theory also explains the outsize role of private actors.

## Limitations acknowledged
The author labels the empirical component a "plausibility probe" — establishing
prima facie utility, not confirmation. Single case, no variation on the outcome.

## Policy recommendations
None substantive. This is a theory article.

## RELEVANCE TO RQ — critical assessment

**Term counts in full text (verified):**
- `offense-defense` / `offense–defense`: **2 occurrences total** (one is the passage
  above; one is the Slayton reference in the bibliography)
- `artificial intelligence`: **0**
- `machine learning`: **0**
- `pre-position`: **0**
- `autonom*`: 5 (none referring to AI autonomy)

**Assessment.** This is the strongest IR-theory article in the corpus and it does
*not* engage AI autonomy at all. It touches offence–defence theory in a single
sentence, and does so to *dismiss* it. It is therefore usable for the RQ in only
one way: as the leading statement of the position that **offence–defence framing is
the wrong lens for cyber**, against which an AI-autonomy argument must be defended.

It cannot support any claim about AI shifting the balance. Any such claim
attributed to Maschmeyer would misrepresent the paper.

**Downstream note.** Maschmeyer's citations point to the offence–defence cyber
literature that this corpus lacks: Slayton (2017, *International Security*),
Gartzke & Lindsay (2015), Libicki (2009), Schneider (2019), Borghard & Lonergan
(2019), Smeets (2022). See corpus-gap note.
