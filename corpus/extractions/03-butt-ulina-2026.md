# Extraction 03 — Butt & Ulina (2026)

## Full citation (APA 7)
Butt, M. S., & Ulina, N. S. (2026). Cyber warfare in the AI era: Emerging attack
trends, defensive strategies and policy challenges, 2022–2026. *The Critical Review
of Social Sciences Studies, 4*(1), 6176–6192.

## Research objective
Systematic examination of digital conflict across national security, geopolitical
competition, and critical infrastructure protection, 2022–2026.

## Stated gap (authors' own)
> "Current literature focuses on individual cyber threats and previous studies have
> not considered offensive Cyber Tactics, AI-driven attacks, ransomware ecosystems,
> disinformation and newly developed Cyber Defence frameworks within today's context."

## Theoretical framework
None explicit. The paper is atheoretical — descriptive/policy-analytic, with no IR
or strategic-studies theory named.

## Methodology
"Evidence-based analytical review model" using secondary sources: peer-reviewed
publications, threat reports, government and institutional documents.

## Cases / datasets
Russia–Ukraine cyber operations; **Volt Typhoon** (CISA 2023 attribution, LOTL
tactics); DPRK cryptocurrency operations. Quantitative trend figures compiled from
IBM Security (2024), ENISA (2024), Verizon (2024), Cybersecurity Ventures (2026).

## MOST IMPORTANT PASSAGE FOR THE RQ — §7.1 "AI-Enabled Autonomous Cyber Operations"
> "Cyber conflict is a significant future threat as AI technologies enable greater
> autonomy. **While fully autonomous offensive cyber weapons are still largely
> theoretical**, AI is being used to aid in reconnaissance, vulnerability discovery,
> phishing, malware development, and the adaptation of cyber exploits. As these
> abilities develop, any future system could recognize targets, determine attack
> routes, and conduct portions of an operation without human participation."
> (Emphasis added. Attributed by the authors to OpenAI, 2024; Schneier, 2023.)

## Offence advantage claim
> "there's still more to come in the new game of innovation on the offensive side,
> including AI-powered attacks, supply chain compromise, attacks on critical
> infrastructure, influence operations, deepfakes and **that's where the defenders
> are behind**."

## Attribution as signalling
> "These declarations carry reputational and even diplomatic costs and **serve as a
> signal of intelligence confidence** (Iasiello, 2023)."

## Principal findings
AI has significantly boosted offensive cyber operations. Zero Trust Architecture,
AI-powered threat intelligence, and quantum-safe cryptography are the emerging
defensive responses. Weaknesses persist in national resilience, international legal
coordination, and public-private partnership.

## Policy recommendations
Tabulated by priority area with responsible actors and timeframes (2025–2030):
workforce development, social media governance, critical infrastructure OT/IT
mandates.

## Limitations acknowledged
None identified in the extracted text. **This is itself a weakness** — a review
making trend claims from vendor-sourced data without discussing source bias.

## RELEVANCE TO RQ — critical assessment

**Term counts (verified):** `Volt Typhoon` 2 | `autonomous` 3 | `escalat*` 5 |
`attribut*` 12 | `signal` 1 | `offense-defense` **0** | `agentic` **0** |
`pre-position` **0**

**Assessment. This is currently the single most relevant paper in the corpus** — the
only one so far that connects AI autonomy and offensive cyber operations in the same
analytical frame, and it also names Volt Typhoon.

Three usable contributions:
1. **A datable claim on the pace question.** "Fully autonomous offensive cyber
   weapons are still largely theoretical" (as of 2026) is a citable anchor for the
   "how fast" clause of the RQ. It positions current autonomy at the
   *sub-task-assistance* level, not the *end-to-end operation* level.
2. **An offence-advantage assertion** ("defenders are behind") — but asserted, not
   demonstrated.
3. **Attribution-as-signal**, though attributed onward to Iasiello (2023), which is
   not in the corpus.

**Serious quality caveats for an international submission.**
- The journal is low-tier and very new (ISSN 3006-7170; Volume 4, 2026). Reviewers at
  a ranked strategic studies journal will not treat it as authoritative.
- **Atheoretical.** No offence–defence theory, no deterrence theory, no signalling
  theory. It cannot bear the theoretical weight of the RQ.
- Trend data is vendor-sourced (IBM, Verizon, Cybersecurity Ventures) with no
  discussion of the well-known incentive problems in vendor threat reporting.
- No limitations section.

**Use it as evidence of the state of practitioner discourse, not as theoretical
authority.** Its value is the §7.1 claim, which is worth quoting precisely because it
is a conservative estimate of autonomy from a source with no incentive to be
conservative.
